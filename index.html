Why MiM Marketing — not MBA, not Masters of Marketing

Know what you're choosing and why

These are different degrees. The Master of Management (Marketing) is a general management degree with a marketing specialisation — that's what I did. The Masters of Marketing Communications / Digital Marketing are separate, more specialist degrees. The MBA needs 3–5+ years of management experience. Australia doesn't really offer a standalone MSc Marketing the way the UK does.

I had about two years of experience in recruitment and marketing — not enough for a competitive MBA. The MiM gave me the academic foundation without the experience barrier. I also got UK offers but chose Australia for better scholarship potential, stronger post-grad work rights, and a more accessible job market. UniMelb was ranked 13th in the world when I applied.

💡 If you have under 3 years of experience, the MiM is almost always the right move over an MBA. Don't rush into an MBA just because it sounds more senior.

The application process

What you need, what actually matters

You need a counsellor for the Australian student visa — the GTE (Genuine Temporary Entrant) assessment is a formal process. I went through Edwise. They handle the visa and process side. Your actual application content — SOP, resume, profile — that has to come from you.

SOP Transcripts IELTS / TOEFL / PTE Resume Reference letters Proof of finances (GTE)

I got both my reference letters from CEOs, not line managers. Go as senior as you genuinely can — it carries more weight.

✏️ What made my application stand out: connecting the dots. Not listing achievements — explaining the why behind each one. Why that job, why this degree, why now, what next. Admissions teams can tell when an application has a coherent story versus a generic list.

Scholarships and funding

How it actually works

For most merit scholarships at UniMelb, you're automatically considered based on your application profile — no separate application needed. Your SOP, academics, extracurriculars, and the clarity of your goals all feed into this. Beyond that, there are grants and bursaries you can actively apply for during your studies. I applied for several and got rejected from most. Keep applying anyway.

  • Strong undergraduate academic record (WAM/GPA)
  • A compelling SOP — your written materials are reviewed
  • Demonstrated extracurriculars and leadership
  • The why — what you'll actually do with this education
  • Check the UniMelb scholarships portal regularly throughout your degree

Course structure, day-to-day, and faculty

What it actually feels like

Subjects per semester

4

Semester length

12 weeks

Timetable

You choose

Teaching is interactive and case-study driven — not passive lectures. The marketing faculty are experienced, approachable, and put in genuine effort. You can challenge them, approach them after class, and they'll engage.

⚠️ Honest curriculum take: some subjects are dated — still anchored in 4Ps and classic brand frameworks when the industry has moved into AI and performance marketing. A few subjects are excellent. Supplement with real-world exposure regardless.

I came from NMIMS, merit list throughout — and WAM here surprised me. Grading varies significantly by instructor, even within the same subject. You can give 100% and not understand why you got a certain mark. Looking at H1 reference assignments from seniors helps. So does talking to people in your specific stream before you enrol.

👥 On group work: don't default to friends for assignments unless you genuinely know they'll deliver. I learned this the hard way — it affected my grades.

Electives — what to pick

Match to your career direction

Don't pick electives because they sound impressive. Pick them because they build the specific skills you'll need to interview for the roles you want.

  • Consulting — dedicated consulting electives exist and are directly relevant
  • Brand / consumer marketing — psychology and consumer behaviour-leaning subjects
  • Digital / data marketing — analytically-oriented subjects, anything with data or digital focus
  • Strategy — mostly covered in core already, you don't need to hunt for it
💡 Talk to someone one year ahead of you in the program who's going into the same field. That's the most useful input you'll get — better than any subject description.

Getting an internship in Australia

How I got ADP ANZ — and what else I tried

I found my internship at ADP ANZ through the UniMelb Business and Economics internship program — one of the best starting points. Companies actively look for students here, and it's more accessible than cold applying. I also got shortlisted for a full-time Apple role in Sydney but couldn't take it alongside studies.

Most Australian internship processes: tailored resume, cover letter, Key Selection Criteria (KSC) questions, one or two conversational interview calls. Be honest and human — not corporate. Know how to talk about your skills and experience clearly.

Skills employers actually want:

Canva Photoshop Power BI Trello Presentation skills Networking ability Data literacy
  • Tailor your resume per role — at minimum rewrite your cover letter and KSC answers
  • Attend industry visits — talk to recruiters there, not just at career fairs
  • Stay in touch with seniors who have jobs — referrals are massively underused
  • Volunteer and organise things — initiative shows here more than in most markets

Living costs in Melbourne

Real numbers, real advice

~$2,000 AUD

Biggest cost

Rent

Best produce market

Queen Vic

Comfortable means: private room, private bathroom, near uni, eating out and socialising every other weekend. Not sharing a room.

🚨 Don't book accommodation before you arrive. The packages marketed to international students are overpriced. Join Melbourne student housing Facebook groups first — much better value.

  • If you do go student accommodation, pick the cheaper options — it's not worth the premium

Student life and finding your people

The social reality

Melbourne is a genuinely good city. There are real opportunities to build community here. But unlike India, it won't come to you. You have to put yourself out there. If you show up and engage, you find good people. If you wait, you won't.

Your social circle will be smaller than you're used to. That's actually okay — the friends you make here tend to be chosen deliberately, and they're usually genuinely good ones.

🛡️ Some people — including people from your own community — will take advantage of newcomers. Take time to observe how people behave before leaning on them. Not pessimism, just preparation.
⏰ International student life is demanding. Studies, cooking, part-time work, social life, job hunting — all at once, without your usual support systems. You will have very little time to yourself. Plan ahead more than you think you need to.
👥

Clubs — GSA, UMSU and the rest

Worth it, with caveats

Yes, get involved. I joined the Graduate Student Association (GSA) and worked directly with the faculty, dean, and associate dean. What you get: access to university leadership, representation experience, student networking events, real connections — and an honorarium (you do get paid). Some of my best contacts came from this.

Getting in is a voting and campaigning process — your pitch and your effort during the campaign genuinely matter. It's competitive.

⚠️ The politics warning: student politics can get ugly. Join for the learning and the people, not for titles. If something feels toxic or is consistently affecting your mental health — leave. No experience is worth that cost.

Job market reality for international students

Honest, not discouraging

Jobs exist. Australia is still a better market than most for international graduates. But it is competitive — not because hiring is hard, but because there are a lot of people going for the same roles. Even casual jobs are competitive.

Typical time to first role

6–8 months

Biggest differentiator

Networking

  • Networking — who you know genuinely matters here more than most places
  • Tailor every application — no generic CVs, no boilerplate cover letters
  • Reach out to recruiters directly on LinkedIn, don't just rely on job boards
  • Keep skills current — AI tools, digital platforms, analytics software
  • Show up consistently at events, in follow-ups, in conversations
💡 The ones who land roles faster almost always started networking in semester one — not after graduation. Start now.
⭐

Does prior work experience matter?

The honest answer is: it depends

Personally: yes. Soft skills, professional confidence, knowing how organisations work — that's real and it shows.

On your Australian resume: mostly no — unless it's Big Four or a globally recognised brand. Overseas experience from smaller companies carries limited weight with local recruiters. You'll often be assessed similarly to someone arriving with none.

✅ Don't delay your start date to stack up more overseas experience. Arrive and prioritise getting Australian experience from day one. Internships, casual work, anything with a local name attached.
✨

Things I wish I'd known

The stuff that doesn't fit elsewhere

  • Start networking in semester one. The people who help you find jobs are people you met 6 months before you needed one.
  • Talk to seniors about instructors before picking subjects. It genuinely changes your academic results.
  • Don't work with friends on group assignments unless you're certain they'll deliver. Be strategic.
  • Attend recordings when you miss lectures — but don't make a habit of it. Falling behind compounds fast.
  • Don't spiral over your WAM. Grading can feel inconsistent. Learn from feedback and move forward.
  • Everyone is figuring it out. The students who look like they have it together mostly don't.
